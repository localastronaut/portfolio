About How I Code

How I code

How I bring my ideas
to life with software.

My goal here is simple: to translate building things with software so that anyone can do it. This isn't overly technical, and I'm not trying to impress anyone. It's just how I build an app or a website that does what I want — using the tools that exist today.

I'm not trying to teach you the "right" way to do this. The AI sometimes builds things that aren't "technically correct," and I'm fine with that. I'd rather build things, and build them fast, than build them perfectly. So this is my whole setup and mindset — the computer, the software, the AI, how I stay organized, and how I share what I make. None of it is complicated, and if you want to start, you can start today.

The mindset

I think about coding like art.

Making an app, to me, is like writing a song. I go looking for inspiration, and sometimes I drop into a flow where I can see the whole thing — and I just bring that vision to life.

01

Build what you'd use yourself

I only build apps I actually want to use. I try to never build something just because I think other people might want it — I've made that mistake too many times. If I wouldn't use it, I don't make it.

02

Chase the vision, not a checklist

The good stuff comes from inspiration and flow, like any creative work. A song isn't a list of features, and neither is an app. I follow the vision and try to make it feel right.

03

Fast and real beats slow and perfect

I'd rather make something real and get it out into the world than polish it forever. "Perfect" is a trap. Done and shared is better than flawless and stuck on my computer.

04

Skip the YouTube rabbit hole

Instead of watching a hundred videos where everyone has a different opinion, just ask the AI to teach you — or to tell you where to start. It's faster, and it's aimed at exactly what you're trying to do.

05

Learn by building, not studying

Building something you actually want, and making mistakes along the way, beats trying to memorize a coding language or a pile of technical junk. The mistakes are the lesson.

The computer

Tools I use

Everyone asks what computer to get. Here's the honest answer: don't overthink it, and don't do a ton of research.

01

I use a Mac

I love it, and it's what I prefer. Honestly, you can use any computer — this is just what I enjoy using every day.

02

Starting out? A MacBook Air

If you're new and building simple stuff, get an M-series MacBook Air. Anything from 2024 onward with an M-series chip will work great.

03

Want more power? Any MacBook Pro

If you want something stronger, grab any MacBook Pro. Don't agonize over the specs. Any MacBook Air or Pro and you'll be just fine.

04

Constraints are good

A laptop's limited space and power actually help: they force you to optimize for the average computer everyone uses — compressing images, storing things in the cloud. Constraints make better software.

If you're using cloud-based AI models, the cloud does all the heavy processing for you, so you really don't need a powerful machine. If you ever go deep into running local models, then performance specs start to matter — but for now, use the cloud stuff and don't overthink it.

What I build with

Software I use

These are the tools I use to actually build and develop software. (The design side — the apps I use to make things look and feel right — will get its own page soon.) You don't need all of them; pick the one for whatever you want to make first.

The apps I build in

Claude Desktop & Codex Desktop

These are the actual apps I spend my time in. I describe what I want in plain English and they write the code — there's more on exactly how I work with them just below.

Apple — iPhone & Mac

Xcode

Apple's free app for building iPhone and Mac apps. I use it because it keeps things simple and it's made by the same people who make the devices. I build for Apple TV here too.

Anything web

The browser

The browser can do almost anything now, and it's amazing for most things. If I want something that works for everyone, I build a website — no app store, no install, just send a link. I mainly use Safari, but Chrome is more developer-friendly, so I'll reach for it occasionally. When I want it to feel like a real installed app, I wrap it with a tool like Tauri.

Android phones

Android Studio

The free tool for building Android apps. When I make something for Android, I build and share it from here. I tend to focus on one platform at a time rather than building for everything at once.

Don't over-analyze every language and platform. If you're new, just start with whatever the AI recommends and learn it. If you hit a wall, find a better solution — that's part of the process. The best way to know what frameworks you actually like is to build things with them and see what worked, what didn't, and what felt right.

The part that changed everything

How I actually work: AI does the heavy lifting.

This is the secret. I'm not typing every line of code from memory. I describe what I want, the AI writes it, and I steer it. This is what makes building open to anyone now.

01

I mostly use Claude

At the moment I mostly use Claude — the desktop app. I used Codex for a while before that. I tell it what I want to build and fix, in plain English, and it does the coding.

02

Talk to it like a developer

Working with AI is a lot like working with a human developer. I describe the goal I'm trying to reach, then ask for its advice on how to get there. Honestly, the less detail you give, the better it often is — it stays goal-oriented, and there's usually a better route than the one you'd have picked.

03

Keep a project "bible"

Lately I keep a living document with all my thoughts on how a project should work — a reference the AI can check to see if it's on track. I'll keep a design file, for example, so when it builds something it follows my design principles instead of guessing or starting from scratch. This has hugely improved building with AI.

04

Ask, build, fix, repeat

When something breaks — and it will — I describe what went wrong and ask how to fix it. When something works, I ask how to make it better. That loop is the whole job.

Staying organized

I'm obsessed with organization.

This is the part most people skip, and I think it's one of the most important. I know where every document I've ever worked on is, and I'm constantly cleaning things up.

01

I know where everything is

I can find almost any file I've ever made — even a paper from my second semester of college — without hunting for it. That means less friction, less frustration, and more time actually building.

02

Organize it yourself, not with AI

Honestly, AI is not good at this. If it renames a file or tries to "organize" things for you, stuff gets lost. Let it build and drive the project's own files however it wants — but your personal files, organize those by hand.

03

A folder system that scales

Don't make so many folders that you get lost in them. Each folder should hold at least a few files. If one gets too full to scan at a glance, it's time to split it; if it only has a file or two, fold it into another.

04

Name things properly

I spend real time naming files correctly and working out a project's structure. Good names and a clear structure save you from chaos later.

05

Plan the structure with the AI

Before building, I ask the AI what systems and structure fit my goals — and where the project could end up. You don't want to build something that can't grow to where you want to take it.

Formats

Get to know your file formats.

It sounds dry, but understanding how files work makes everything smoother — especially when you're working with AI.

01

Know what each one is for

Understand the difference between, say, a PDF and a Word doc — and why you'd reach for one over the other. A little of this knowledge goes a long way.

02

AI loves plain text

AI works best with plain text and markup, so I try to write everything in plain text or Markdown, and use HTML and CSS to lay it out visually.

03

Stick to open formats

I stick to open formats like PDF, CSV, JSON, TXT, and MD. I avoid Word and PowerPoint — they need proprietary software. If I truly need a document or a deck I'll use Mac's Pages, Keynote, or Numbers, but that's rare; the AI can make most of this itself.

What I focus on

Stability and simplicity — then get it out fast.

It's tempting to keep piling on features. I try hard not to. Here's where I actually put my energy.

01

Not more features

Adding features forever is endless and causes most of the problems. I'd rather have something small that works than something big that breaks.

02

Keep it as simple as possible

I aim for the fewest features, clicks, and settings to get the job done. Simple and dependable wins every time.

03

Simple enough for a stranger

If someone using it for the first time instantly understands it, it's almost always a better tool for me too. Building for a newcomer makes the whole thing better.

04

Ship early, then listen

The most important thing is getting it to real people as fast as possible for feedback. I never guess what people will want — I build what I'd want, then let their feedback shape it.

Saving your work

GitHub, explained simply.

GitHub is where I store every coding project, and it's worth learning well because it quietly saves you from disaster. Think of it as a time machine and a safe for your code — every change is recorded, so if something breaks you can roll back to when it worked.

Two things to know. First, GitHub is for code, not big files — large things like video, music, and images will actually break it, so keep those on a server or locally and list them in a .gitignore file so GitHub skips them. Second, it's incredible for working with other people: you can bring someone on and collaborate without stepping on each other. That's what it does best. Learn it well so you can use it well.

Why it's worth it

Go back in time

Broke something that was working an hour ago? Roll back to the good version. Nothing is ever truly lost.

Why it's worth it

Made for collaboration

Bring someone on and build together without overwriting each other's work. This is GitHub at its very best.

Why it's worth it

Code here, big files elsewhere

Code lives on GitHub; big media lives on a server or locally, kept out with a .gitignore. Loading large files will break it, so don't.

Storage

Where the rest of your files live.

Code goes on GitHub. Everything else — personal files, media, project storage — needs a home too.

01

Projects → AWS

When a project needs real storage — a database, big files, anything that has to live online — I use Amazon AWS. It's where the heavier project stuff goes.

02

Personal files → iCloud

My personal files live in iCloud. I trust Apple's security and stability more than anyone else's — other services have deleted my files over the years, and iCloud never has.

03

Personal & sharing → Google

I also lean on Google for some personal storage and email, and on Google Drive to share files and send people links to them.

Getting it to other people

Sharing what you build.

Building something is only half of it. Here's how I get a finished thing into someone else's hands, depending on what it is.

01

iPhone → TestFlight, then the App Store

I share early builds through TestFlight to get feedback, then release on the App Store. iPhone apps have to go through the store, and you'll need an Apple developer account.

02

Mac → TestFlight or a DMG

On Mac you can install apps without the App Store, which makes releasing much easier. I'll use TestFlight or just ship a DMG, depending on how complex the app is.

03

Android → APK or Google Play

For Android I'll just share an APK file directly, or put it on the Google Play Store, which has a beta-testing setup like TestFlight.

04

Websites → GitHub Pages or AWS

Basic sites I host free on GitHub Pages. For something more complex I use Amazon AWS — it's a bit more involved, so I lean on the AI to break it down.

You'll need an Apple developer account to release through TestFlight or the App Store — and to sign your apps so they aren't flagged as a possible threat on people's computers.

For domain names — the web address people type in — I've used a Squarespace domain, though I may move away from it because of some issues I've run into. The domain is just the address; the hosting (like GitHub Pages or AWS) is the building it points to.

Play it safe

Make sure what you're building is legal.

Try not to use other people's copyrighted work, and make sure you understand the licenses on the dependencies and frameworks you're using. Do your best to learn this stuff as you go — but don't get overwhelmed or scared off by it.

Keeping track of ideas

Where I keep my notes.

Ideas, plans, and to-dos need a home too. Right now I use Apple Notes because it's simple and on every device, and I'm testing Obsidian to see if it fits the way I think.

The only advice that matters

Just start building, and ask the chat how to do it better and how to fix things when they break.

You won't understand everything at first, and that's fine — you learn by making real things. Pick one small idea you'd actually use, open a chat, and ask it to help you build it. When it breaks, ask why. When it works, ask how to make it better. Then get it in front of people. That's the whole secret — anyone can do it, including you.
